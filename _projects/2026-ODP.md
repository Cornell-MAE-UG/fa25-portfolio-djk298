<h3>Client &amp; Stakeholders</h3>

<p>
  <strong>Cornell CALS Extension</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp;
  <strong>E&amp;J Gallo Winery</strong> &nbsp;·&nbsp;
  <strong>National Grape</strong>

<h3>The Problem</h3>

<p>
  Grape farmers in the Northeast US are threatened by Spotted Lanternflies (SLFs) that infiltrate
  harvests. The SLF population grows rapidly — many nymphs survive to adulthood, where they
  reproduce abundantly, increasing their population by a factor of <strong>5.47</strong>. Current
  solutions primarily target adults, but by that stage rapid population growth has already occurred,
  increasing severity regardless of intervention.
</p>
<p>
  Decreasing the SLF reproduction factor below 5.47 would slow population increase and potentially
  stagnate it in a given area, reducing the number of SLFs per vine from the current level of
  approximately 400.
</p>

<h3>Proposed Solution 1 — The Scraper</h3>

<p>
  The Scraper is a device that makes it quicker and simpler to remove SLF egg masses and improves
  accessibility in hard-to-reach places. The user spots egg masses visually (or using an integrated
  light feature), extends the scraper via a telescoping mechanism, and removes the egg masses from
  the surface into the scraper housing.
</p>

<p><em>Why it's better than the status quo:</em></p>
<ul>
  <li>Current SLF scrapers are credit-card-sized and require close contact with egg masses</li>
  <li>Our scraper makes the process simpler and improves overall accessibility</li>
</ul>

<p>
  For our MVP, we will create a prototype that extends and contains egg masses along with a
  preliminary UI. Long-term goals include a working camera system and reproducible manufacturing.
</p>

<h3>Proposed Solution 2 — Nymph Netting</h3>

<p>
  Our second solution involves weaving netting in a spiral formation to funnel and trap nymph SLFs
  that are climbing trees to feed (nymphs cannot fly). This specifically targets the nymph stage,
  preventing them from reaching adulthood. The design incorporates recent research showing that SLF
  nymphs are attracted to high-frequency vibrations, which can be used to draw them into the trap.
</p>

<h3>Key Risks &amp; Unknowns</h3>
<ul>
  <li>
    <strong>Ecological impact of netting:</strong> The netting may unintentionally affect other
    wildlife. We plan to mitigate this by testing smaller-scale traps with different entry funnel
    configurations.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>User adoption of the scraper:</strong> Even an effective scraper will have no impact
    without consistent use. We will conduct user surveys to prioritize feedback and iterate on
    the design accordingly.
  </li>
</ul>

<h3>Questions for the Client</h3>

<ol>
  <li>
    <strong>What are your biggest gripes with current scrapers used to remove egg masses?</strong><br>
    <em>Decision affected:</em> This will enable us to improve user comfort and efficiency — we can
    modify the scraper shape or material to better handle specific textures or geometries.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>What traps are currently in use — how do they lure SLFs, and are they effective?</strong><br>
    <em>Decision affected:</em> We can learn from existing approaches to avoid ideas that don't work
    in practice and refine our netting design accordingly.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>What are the main barriers to removing egg masses: height, surface geometry, or consistency?</strong><br>
    <em>Decision affected:</em> Knowing this will let us design the scraper geometry for as many
    situations as possible and clarify which features to prioritize.
  </li>
</ol>

<h3>Purpose of the Prototype</h3>

<p>
  The functional prototype was built to test our core mechanical systems — the springs allowing for
  deflection of the scraper blade, the spring hinge that allows the scraper to change angles, and an
  ability to move the blade to reach sharp corners and crevices. The goal was not a finished product,
  but a working proof-of-concept that could generate real data to inform the next design iteration.
</p>

<h3>What Was Tested</h3>

<p>We ran three design tests:</p>

<p>
  <strong>Test 1 — Blade Angle Conformity.</strong> The angle of the scraper blade must vary to conform to different surfaces. Ideally, it can vary to a radius of curvature of 4”. This tests the scraper blade itself, springs, and spring hinge mechanism. To conduct this test, we will measure the displacement of the tip of the scraper blade from its initial position to its fully ‘bent’ position. This will allow us to calculate the angle and determine how much the ends of the blade are able to conform. 
</p>

<p>
  <strong>Test 2 — User Comfort &amp; Force Transfer.</strong> The force a user must apply to the
  scraper blade should fall within a comfortable range. We had 10 participants scrape modeled egg
  masses off both flat and curved surfaces, then rate their comfort on a scale of 1–10. This tests the scraper blade and the length/general sizing of the handle. This will be especially useful as we move towards modeling and understanding the telescoping handle in the future. To test this, we will have 10 people try to scrape off modeled egg masses. Each participant will rank the ease and comfort of the force they are applying so that the handle can be modeled for the average person’s strength.
</p>

<p>
  <strong>Test 3 — Cyclic Durability.</strong> Through cyclic use, the scraper blade must not experience significant deformation beyond ¼”. This tests the scraper blade and the spring mechanisms. This ensures that with repeated use, the mechanism does not degrade and does not apply a force on the blade such that it enters the plastic deformation region. To test this, we will take measurements and record the scraper blade and spring displacement before any scraping. Then, we will scrape off modeled egg masses 40 times and retake these measurements to see if the scraper blade has deformed and if the springs have changed. We will also visually inspect the blade and mechanism for any visible damage. 
</p>

<h3>Results &amp; Outcomes</h3>

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Test 1 — Blade Angle Conformity:</strong> We measured that the center of the tip of the scraper can deflect at most about 0.25” when pressed against a smooth round surface. Using that measurement and the fact that the two edges of the PVC blade are 4 ¼” apart, we can calculate that the minimum radius of curvature our scraper can wrap around is about 9.16”. This is constrained by the mounting and flexibility of our blade. From this test, we can conclude that the placement of the springs/bolts and the flexibility of the blade are insufficient, as they don’t allow the blade to wrap around surfaces with our benchmark radius of curvature. We will likely keep a similar design and dimensions, but change the thickness of the scraper blade to allow for more flexibility in the blade itself.
</div>

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Test 2 — User Comfort &amp; Force Transfer:</strong> On flat surfaces, all 10 users rated
  the experience as comfortable. On curved surfaces, however, 9 out of 10 found it required
  uncomfortable force to bend the 1/16" acrylic blade against the surface before scraping. Users
  preferred making multiple flat passes over a single conforming pass. This points to the blade
  being too stiff — the next prototype will use a thinner ~1/32" blade and a larger handle to reduce
  required force.
</div>

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Test 3 — Cyclic Durability:</strong> After 40 scraping cycles, no meaningful deformation
  was observed. The blade and springs returned to their original positions, with deflection under
  1/32" — well within the ¼" threshold. The materials proved durable enough to carry forward into
  future prototypes, though we will re-run this test after changing the blade thickness.
</div>

<h3>Next Steps</h3>

<p>
  Based on these results, our priorities for the next prototype are:
</p>
<ul>
  <li>Reduce blade thickness from 1/16" to ~1/32" acrylic to improve flexibility on curved surfaces</li>
  <li>Scale up the handle size and work toward a telescoping handle design to reduce user effort</li>
  <li>Re-run durability testing with the thinner blade to confirm it holds up over repeated use</li>
  <li>Test on a wider variety of outdoor surfaces (tree bark, fence posts, etc.) for more realistic data</li>
</ul>

<h3>Context and Problem Statement</h3>

<p>
The spotted lanternfly presents an especially challenging problem as its population grows rapidly due to a high number of nymphs surviving to adulthood, where they reproduce abundantly, increasing their population by a factor of 5.47 [1]. Current solutions primarily target adults, but rapid population growth is already occurring by this stage, increasing the severity of their impact regardless.
</p>

<p>
This aspect is promising as it tackles the root issue: limiting SLF populations by reducing reproduction success. We aim to reduce the overall population of SLFs in a vineyard or region. Creating even a small dent in SLF’s reproduction rate can have a significant impact on their numbers and harm.
</p>

<h3>Final Prototype and Application</h3>

<p>
Our final prototype is an extendable scraper with a conforming blade that matches the curvature of a surface by utilizing 3-point bending. The scraper is lightweight and has a telescoping handle, allowing it to be used at multiple lengths. The blade is attached to a collection pouch that egg masses fall into when scraped, and the handle itself uses a spring hinge mechanism to vary its angle. Additionally, there are several orientations of the handle, allowing the user to vary its angle with respect to two planes.
</p>

<p>
The scraper is meant to be an accessible and easy method for users and the general public to scrape egg masses off of trees and other varied surfaces, thus reducing the SLF population and the infestation issue for harvesters. This could be used in vineyards and farms, where SLFs are especially troublesome. Additionally, the scrapers could be incentivized and placed in parks for the general public.
</p>

<p>
The user workflow is as follows: A user spots an SLF egg mass, extends the scraper using the telescoping handle, scrapes the egg masses using the compliant blade, and finally the egg masses fall into the collection pouch for easy disposal and removal.
</p>

<h3>Conclusion and Recommendation</h3>

<p>
This design has potential, but it requires more in-depth updates and field testing. The scraper would be more effective if outfitted with a light, a camera, and a display that would aid users in viewing egg masses, and the telescoping could be automated in future iterations. Our tests proved the scraper could withstand heavy usage and conform to different surfaces, but showed issues with the disposable bag. In the future the disposable plastic bag should be replaced with a permanent container housing disposable liners, and the scraper should be tested with environmental factors (e.g. weather).
</p>

<p>
However, even with this design, it is hard to guarantee that people would use the scraper without a larger incentive. Furthermore, it is more expensive than anticipated to implement this, as vineyards would have to hire someone to scrape off the egg masses. More research and studies should be done into existing incentives numbers i.e. how many people use the SLF egg mass credit cards vs how many are handed out. Surveys could be conducted on this to discern if this is truly a promising product. This could affect future implementations of this product, and ways to reduce the production cost should also be considered (whether mass producing them or switching to certain lower-cost parts).
</p>

<h3>Testing and Results</h3>

<p>
We aimed to create a scraper that is quick to use, can durably conform to varying surfaces, is comfortable to the average user, and:
</p>

<ul>
  <li>It should be able to be set up and used in under 30 seconds. Time is a high priority.</li>
  <li>It should be able to withstand repeated use, maintaining its functionality by not deforming more than ¼” after 50 times of use. Durability is a high priority.</li>
  <li>It should be able to bend to a minimum radius of curvature of 4 inches to allow it to follow many different surface curvatures for ease of removal. Adapting to different surfaces is a high priority.</li>
  <li>It should require a comfortable level of force for the average user. At least 75% of users must rate their comfort with the force exerted to scrape off ‘egg masses’ at least a 7/10 on flat and curved surfaces. A reasonable comfort level is a high priority.</li>
  <li>It should collect egg masses consistently in the collection pouch. At least 75% of scraped egg masses should fall into the pouch, and it should not overflow or deteriorate within 15 scrapings.</li>
</ul>

<p>
Our earlier prototypes required a lot of force from users despite being able to curve to a large radius, which led us to use a thinner, 1/32” scraper blade. Additionally, we sized up the handle radius to 1-¼”. The final prototype was able to be set up within 15 seconds when replacing the bag (took &lt;5 without replacement), did not show any signs of degradation or deformation after a full exhibition or repeated testing, had a minimum radius of curvature of 9.16”, and was rated highly by users as seen in the chart below. We tested it on trees outside, and it scraped modeled egg masses reasonably well with gravity aiding this, as we struggled to collect our egg masses properly when scraping horizontally during demonstrations. Thus, as seen in this testing, the scraper is easy to set up, durable, conforms to a variety of surfaces, and is comfortable for users.
</p>

<p>
However, the scraper struggled with collecting egg masses. The disposable pouch only caught egg masses about 50% of the time when tested on trees and seemed flimsy at times. Future iterations of this product should either implement support for the disposable pouch or replace it with a permanent container housing replaceable liners.
</p>

<h3>Prototype and Testing Details</h3>

<p>
Our first “proof of concept” prototype is shown here and was constructed from cardboard, wood, and paper. This prototype helped us discern estimates for the blade size and handle length and identify problems that arise during physical assembly, such as positioning of the disposable collection pouch.
</p>

<p>
We moved from this to our first real prototype, where our main goal was to troubleshoot the scraper blade and test our 3 point bending mechanism, spring hinge, and scraper blade variance. We created CAD as a mockup and constructed the scraper as pictured here. For this prototype, we stress tested it over 40 times to determine if the blade would deflect, had users rate their comfort on flat and curved surfaces with modeled egg masses, and calculated our maximum radius of curvature. The blade experienced negligible deflection, there was no degradation, and the maximum radius of curvature was well above the required 4”. However, users struggled to scrape egg masses on curved surfaces. We also found difficulty in the range of motion with the blade itself, as the spring hinge created a tendency to ‘snap’ back after scraping was done.
</p>

<p>
Prototype 2 focused on the telescoping handle: making it work, figuring out handle connectors, moving to a new handle size, and testing this. We ordered 2 aluminum hollow rods & 2 different types of handle connectors to determine which was more ideal. Through this, we experimented and found a way to make the rods telescope, and test users unanimously agreed the lever locking mechanism was more intuitive than the knob locking mechanism. We tested the telescoping over 50 uses and saw no degradation during this period.
</p>

<p>
Our final prototype used a 1/32” CPVC scraper blade, 3 aluminum rods with 2 telescoping connectors, foam to softly stop the scraper blade from ‘snapping’ back, a large disposable bag, lasercut handle connector and triangular handle piece, 3D printer converter to take the circular rod to rectangular to connect to the blade assembly, the same spring hinge, and similar ¼”-20 and M3 hardware. This prototype withstood 50 uses in testing and subsequent use during presentations and exhibition day without significant degradation or deflection, was tested on trees outside, was rated highly across all users on comfort on both curved and flat surfaces, and had a maximum radius of curvature of 9.16”.
</p>

<h3>Assembly Instructions</h3>

<ol>
  <li>Assembling the Scraper Blade
    <ul>
      <li>Cut the 1' x 1' CPVC Plate to 4¼" x 2¾" using band saw, belt sand at a 30° angle</li>
      <li>Assemble springs onto ¼”-20 bolts and bolt through the laser-cut handle connector and CPVC scraper blade, placing a nut on the other end</li>
      <li>Torque the nut to allow for compression while keeping the assembly secure</li>
    </ul>
  </li>

  <li>Assembling the Angling Head
    <ul>
      <li>Hold the spring hinge open and align it with corresponding holes in the laser-cut pieces</li>
      <li>Ensure the hinge folds so the blade goes into the Triangular Handle Piece</li>
      <li>Connect to the Handle Connector & Triangular Handle Piece using M3 hardware</li>
      <li>Torque nuts tightly with a small wrench</li>
    </ul>
  </li>

  <li>Assembling the telescoping handle
    <ul>
      <li>Wrap 3 layers of tape on the bottom of the Telescoping Rods to create a makeshift stop</li>
      <li>Place the Large Telescoping Connector onto the Large Telescoping Rod</li>
      <li>Slide the Medium Rod through the bottom of the Large Rod</li>
      <li>Tighten bolts until rod moves but holds position</li>
      <li>Repeat for the Small Telescoping Connector</li>
    </ul>
  </li>

  <li>Attaching the Angling Head to the handle
    <ul>
      <li>Press the 3D Printed Handle Converter onto the Small Telescoping Rod</li>
      <li>Align and secure with ¼”-20 bolts</li>
      <li>Be careful not to ‘snap’ the blade</li>
      <li>Place foam on top as a soft stop</li>
    </ul>
  </li>

  <li>Attach the disposable bag to the back of the blade assembly</li>
</ol>
